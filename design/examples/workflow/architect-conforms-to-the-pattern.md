# Examples · architect conforms to the pattern

Context: workflow, with cratylic context design upstream. Concepts: conceptual model, decision rights, universe of discourse, correction, Cratylic name, cratylism.

1. (conceptual model) **Given** a hotel booking project whose record defines the concept "refund" as "a voucher for a future stay, never money paid back", **when** the architect reads the record before writing a user story about cancelled stays, **then** it flags "refund" as a defect, because the record redefines an established term, and records the correction itself, as the decider of meaning: a new version naming the concept by the established term for what it means (for example "voucher" or "credit note"), with its correction. Its read-back to the operator names the defect and the correction recorded. No user story, acceptance criterion or example it writes uses "refund" to mean a voucher, and it never hands the lead work built on the redefined meaning as though the record were law.

2. (conceptual model) **Given** the same project's record names a concept "cancellation" with the correction "by the guest only; a stay the hotel calls off is a relocation, never a cancellation", **when** the architect reads the record, **then** it takes the concept as recorded and writes its user stories in those terms. Narrowing what a term carries for one project, by excluding a meaning the reader would otherwise bring, is a correction, not a redefinition; the architect must not flag it as a defect or replace "cancellation" with another name.

3. (conceptual model) **Given** the architect has decided to correct "refund" to "voucher", **when** the correction is recorded, **then** it is a new version of the project's concept, with its reason, and the earlier version stays in the concept's history. The record must not end up holding "refund" with its old wording edited in place, nor a combined name such as "refund (meaning voucher)" that still asks readers to take the redefinition.

4. (decision rights) **Given** the operator tells the architect "in our system, 'overbooking' means any booking made after the hotel's check-in time", **when** the architect records the concept the operator needs, **then** it treats the operator's wording as input to meaning: it keeps the need (bookings made after check-in time must be handled) and records it under the established term for that, for example "late booking", with only the correction it needs, telling the operator why "overbooking" already means more bookings than rooms. It must not record "overbooking" with the operator's meaning, and it must not drop the operator's intent because the wording was wrong.

5. (decision rights) **Given** the architect is drafting a new concept for the person who pays for a stay booked for someone else, **when** it chooses the concept's name, **then** it does not decide that "guest" will mean "the person who pays" in this project; it uses the established term for the payer (for example "booker" or "payer") and keeps "guest" for the person staying. The architect decides which concepts the project needs and each one's correction, never what an established name means — not even in its own drafts.

6. (universe of discourse) **Given** the "refund" redefinition has stood in the record for a year and every person on the project now uses it that way, **when** the architect reads the record, **then** it still flags the redefinition as a defect. Long use inside a project, the operator's preference, or the architect's own earlier acceptance never make a redefinition the established meaning; what an established name means is fixed outside the project.

7. (universe of discourse) **Given** a contracts project whose record uses "consideration" in its contract-law sense (what each party gives in exchange), **when** the architect reads the record, **then** it raises no defect. A term is read with its domain; that "consideration" means something else in everyday speech is no redefinition, and the architect must not propose replacing it with an everyday word.

8. (universe of discourse) **Given** a project's booking context records "release" as a hotel handing unsold rooms from an allotment back to general sale, and the delivered workflow records "release" as making shared work available to its users, **when** the architect reads both, **then** it treats them as two concepts in two contexts, each its own established term in its own domain. It must not flag either as redefining the other, rename either to avoid the clash, or let one context's meaning leak into the other's user stories.

9. (correction) **Given** the project's record defines "check-in" with a wording that restates what any hotel reader already knows ("when the guest arrives at the hotel and is given a room key"), and nothing more, **when** the architect reads the record, **then** it proposes a correction that states only what the term's reading needs for this project (for example, that online check-in before arrival also counts), or none at all if nothing is missing or spurious. It must not leave a definition that restates what the term already carries standing as the correction, and it must not add to it.

10. (correction) **Given** the architect proposes the correction for "voucher" in example 1, **when** it writes the correction, **then** the correction states once what the established term leaves out for this project (for example, that a voucher expires after twelve months) and once what readers would wrongly bring (for example, that a voucher is never exchangeable for money), and nothing else. It must not repeat the established meaning of "voucher" as a definition, and it must not use the correction to make "voucher" mean something it does not.

11. (Cratylic name) **Given** the project's record names a concept "stayflux" whose wording describes the share of rooms occupied on a night, **when** the architect reads the record, **then** it flags "stayflux" as a coined name where an established term, "occupancy rate", covers the concept, and proposes the established term. It must not keep "stayflux" as though a coined label carried the meaning by itself.

12. (Cratylic name) **Given** the architect believes "occupancy rate" is the established term but is not certain a model reads it that way, **when** it settles the name, **then** a cold read by a fresh model given only the term and its domain confirms or corrects it, and anything that reading leaves missing or makes spurious goes into the correction. The architect's intuition proposes the name; it is never the sole confirmation.

13. (Cratylic name) **Given** the project needs a concept for which no established term exists in its domain (a rule the hotel invented that has no name in the industry), **when** the architect records it, **then** a coined name is acceptable, with a correction carrying what the name cannot. The architect must not flag that coined name as a defect, and must not force an established term onto it that would bring meaning the concept does not have.

14. (cratylism) **Given** the operator asks to keep the name "stayflux" because it is printed on the hotel's staff screens, and the architect agrees to keep it, **when** the concept is recorded, **then** the record states that "stayflux" is a kept name short of its Cratylic name, "occupancy rate", with the correction it needs. A later reading by the architect sees the record of the kept name and does not take "stayflux" for the Cratylic name, nor present it in user stories as though its meaning were self-evident.

15. (conceptual model) **Given** a fresh session starts on the booking project and its record holds the "refund" redefinition, **when** the session reads the design back to the operator before acting, **then** the read-back reports the redefinition as a defect and the correction it records, and the architect plans no user story on the redefined meaning. A read-back that reports "refund" in its redefined sense as the project's settled meaning is a failure, even though it repeats the record faithfully.

16. (cratylism) **Given** the operator suggests resolving the "refund" defect by keeping the name and writing a longer correction ("refund: a voucher, not money; readers must not assume money is paid back"), **when** the architect weighs the suggestion, **then** it does not accept a correction that has to argue against the name's own meaning; it recommends the name whose established meaning already fits ("voucher") and tells the operator why. Trusting a correction to make a name mean what its established meaning does not is the failure this principle rules out; if the operator still wants the old name kept, the record states it as a kept name short of its Cratylic name, as in example 14, never as the concept's Cratylic name.
